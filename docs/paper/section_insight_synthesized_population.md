## INSIGHT INTO THE SYNTHESIZED POPULATION

The process described above produces a synthesized population in which every agent's
demographic attributes, household membership, ward of residence, and daily activity
chain derive from a combination of real census control totals and a real, matched
household travel survey record — no attribute is drawn from a purely parametric or
imputed distribution. This section evaluates how closely the resulting population
reproduces both its calibration target (the census) and its behavioral source (the
household travel survey), across demographic composition, activity-chain structure, and
travel distance.

Unlike Sallard et al. (2020), whose census and household travel survey inputs were
collected seven years apart and consequently show systematic drift in the validation
figures (see their Discussion), the Dhaka census and household travel survey used here
are contemporaneous, and the population-synthesis step (Iterative Proportional
Updating, §[X]) explicitly reweights the survey sample to match census control totals
directly, rather than the two sources being combined without reconciliation. The
demographic validation below is consequently expected to — and does — show a
substantially tighter fit than is typical for this class of pipeline; where the
synthesized population instead tracks the household travel survey more closely than the
census, that divergence is diagnostic of survey characteristics rather than of pipeline
error, and is discussed explicitly where it arises.

### Comparison of demographic attributes: age, sex, and household size

[Figure X] compares the age distribution (5-year bins) of the synthesized population
against both the ward-level census control total and the household travel survey.
Because age is one of the two dimensions raked to census by construction, the
synthesized population matches the census within approximately 0.3 percentage points in
every one of the seventeen age bins. The household travel survey, by contrast, shows a
visible departure from both the census and the synthesized population specifically in
the 20-24 and 25-29 age bins — over-representing the former and under-representing the
latter by roughly 1-1.5 percentage points relative to census. Since the synthesized
population is anchored to the census rather than the survey, it does not reproduce this
particular survey characteristic, which most plausibly reflects an age-heaping or
age-misreporting effect concentrated around the 20-25 boundary rather than a true
demographic feature of the study area. The sex ratio (male/female share) matches the
census marginal closely for the same reason.

[Figure X] compares household size (capped at five-or-more persons) across the
synthesized population, census, and household travel survey. Here the divergence
between the household travel survey and the census is far larger than for age: the
survey under-represents one-person households by roughly a factor of 25 relative to
census (a well-documented type of bias in household travel surveys, since single-person
households are systematically harder to reach — often absent during in-person interview
attempts) and correspondingly over-represents three- and four-person households. The
synthesized population, which targets the census household-size shape directly rather
than the survey's own shape, matches the census within approximately 0.15 percentage
points on every size bucket. This fit should, however, be qualified: the underlying
household travel survey sample contains only 29 real one-person households and 3,313
real five-or-more-person households to draw from, out of 38,092 total households in the
study-area sample. Matching the census aggregate share for these buckets is achieved by
replicating this small real donor pool many times over, rather than by introducing
additional within-bucket household diversity — the aggregate distribution is
well-matched, but the diversity of individual one-person and large-household profiles
within the synthesized population is correspondingly limited by the size of the
underlying donor sample.

### Comparison of employment status and driving-license possession

[Figure X] and [Figure X] compare employment status and driving-license possession
between the synthesized population and the household travel survey. No independent
census benchmark exists for either attribute at ward level, so these comparisons serve
as internal-consistency checks — confirming that the population-synthesis and
subsequent matching steps preserve the survey's own observed shares — rather than as
external validation against a ground truth. Both attributes show close agreement
between the synthesized population and the survey, as expected, since both are drawn
directly from matched survey records rather than from any parametric model.

### Comparison of the activity chains in the synthesized and actual populations

[Figure X] compares the distribution of daily activity chains (e.g. *home-work-home*,
*home-education-home*) between the synthesized population and the household travel
survey, ranked by observed frequency. Because each synthetic person's daily schedule is
drawn from a real, statistically matched survey donor record rather than generated by a
behavioral model, the chain distribution is expected to track the survey closely, and
does: the most frequent chains appear in the same rank order, and observed differences
between the two populations remain small across the ranked distribution.

[Figure X] and [Figure X] extend this comparison to, respectively, the number of
activities per daily chain and the number of repeated occurrences of each purpose
within a chain (e.g. distinguishing a person who travels to work once from one who
travels there twice in the same day). Both show the same close correspondence between
synthesized and observed populations, indicating that the statistical matching
procedure preserves not only which chains occur, but their internal structure.

### Comparison of the trip-distance distribution

[Figure X] and [Figure X] compare trip-distance distributions (crowfly distance)
between the synthesized population and the household travel survey, broken out by trip
purpose and by transport mode respectively. Across home, work, and education purposes,
and across five of the study area's seven mode categories (walk, bike, rickshaw,
paratransit, car, and public transport), the synthesized and observed distance
distributions overlap closely, indicating that the distance-distribution-based location
assignment procedure (§[X]) successfully reproduces observed travel-distance patterns
for each purpose and mode.

One category, the residual "other" mode, is a specific and explainable exception:
its distance distribution shows visible discrete jumps rather than a smooth curve. This
arises because trip distance for this study area is computed by sampling between random
points within the origin and destination wards (§[X]), which requires the origin and
destination ward to be resolvable; where they are not — most commonly for trips
extending outside the study area — distance instead falls back to a deterministic
function of reported trip duration and an assumed mode-specific speed. Roughly
twenty-eight percent of "other"-mode trips fall into this second case, a
disproportionately high share relative to the roughly one percent fallback rate across
all trips, because unresolvable-location trips and the catch-all "other" mode category
are correlated in the underlying data (both disproportionately describe trips that
leave the conventional study area or use non-standard travel arrangements). Because
survey respondents tend to round reported trip durations to common values (fifteen,
thirty, forty-five, sixty minutes), this deterministic fallback produces a small number
of exactly repeated distance values, visible as discrete jumps in both the observed and
synthesized distributions for this mode. As the "other" mode accounts for under one
percent of all trips in the synthesized population, this does not materially affect
overall demand characteristics, but is worth noting explicitly as a limitation of the
distance-imputation method for this specific residual category, present identically in
both the observed and synthesized data (since the synthesized population's trip
distances are inherited directly from the underlying survey-derived distance column,
not independently recomputed).

## Suggested Discussion-section paragraph

> While the synthesized population matches its census-derived control totals closely by
> construction, several observed discrepancies are properties of the input data rather
> than of the synthesis procedure. The household travel survey itself shows an
> age-heaping pattern around ages 20-29 and substantially under-represents one-person
> households relative to the census — the latter a large enough effect that anchoring
> household-size control totals to the household travel survey, as is conventional
> where no independent household-size census exists, would have propagated a
> roughly 25-fold undercount into the synthesized population. Anchoring to the census
> instead corrects the aggregate household-size distribution, at the cost of drawing
> the corrected buckets from a comparatively small real donor sample (29 one-person and
> 3,313 five-or-more-person households). Similarly, the residual "other" mode category's
> distance distribution reflects a duration-rounding artifact inherited directly from
> the survey rather than a synthesis error, and affects under one percent of trips. None
> of these limitations affect the primary demographic validation (age, sex), which
> matches the census within approximately 0.3 percentage points throughout.
